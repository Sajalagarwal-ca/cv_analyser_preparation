The Complete CPG / FMCG KPI Playbook

From primary sales to household panel — the metrics, data sources, and analytical frameworks that actually run the FMCG business. A consolidation of my four-part series into a single, practitioner-ready guide.

CPG FMCG KPIs Nielsen Household Panel Analytics

Who this is for: Brand managers, category managers, sales analysts, and data scientists working with consumer-goods companies (or partnering with them). If you have ever stared at a Nielsen report and wondered which two numbers actually matter — this is your map.

Why I Wrote This Guide

In FMCG, more decisions are killed by wrong metric interpretation than by wrong strategy. A brand manager quotes market share, sales says primary is up, Nielsen says offtake is down, panel says penetration is flat — and everyone is technically correct. The problem is not the data; it is that most people never learn how the pieces fit together.

This article walks through the entire measurement stack in the order a practitioner actually uses it: the vocabulary, then the six data sources, then the internal-vs-retail reconciliation, and finally the household-panel deep dive that separates strategic brand managers from tactical ones.

Part 1 · The Glossary Every FMCG Analyst Must Know

Before we get to KPIs, we need shared vocabulary. Skip this section only if you already speak fluent trade.

The three types of "sales"

  • Primary Sales — from the company to the distributor. This is what the finance team calls "turnover."
  • Secondary Sales — from the distributor to the retailer. This is what most sales-force targets are actually built on, because this is what reaches the shelf.
  • Consumption Sales / Offtake — from the retailer to the end consumer. The company cannot directly measure this in traditional trade, so it is estimated by market-research agencies (Nielsen being the industry default). Nielsen samples a subset of traditional-trade stores, extrapolates, and reports share. Accuracy is imperfect — but in the absence of anything better, this is what every brand manager benchmarks against. For high-value goods (makeup, durables), companies often track counter-wise offtake themselves.

Trade Schemes

Schemes are the levers a company pulls to push volume through the trade — retailers, wholesalers, and distributors. They come in two shapes and two flavors.

By structure

  • Quantity Purchase Scheme (QPS): discounts tied to units bought.
    Example: 144 pieces → 8%, 72 pieces → 6%, 48 pieces → 4%, 24 pieces → 2%.
  • Value Purchase Scheme (VPS): discounts tied to invoice value.
    Example: ₹10,000 → 8%, ₹8,000 → 6%, ₹6,000 → 4%, ₹4,000 → 2%.

By who receives it

  • Primary Schemes — deducted at the invoice level to the distributor. Effectively an extra margin for the distributor.
  • Secondary Schemes — the distributor is expected to first extend the scheme to the market and then claim it back from the company. This is where rate ka chakkar (rate leakage / arbitrage) usually creeps in and quietly erodes margin.

Ground-level sales terms

  • Beat — the route a salesman follows on a given day, planned so every store in the territory is covered at the required frequency (e.g., once a week).
  • FOC — Free of Cost (samples, giveaways, incentive stock).
  • Display / FSU — paid shelf space; in Modern Trade, a floor-standing unit.
  • Strike Rate — % of successful sales calls out of total calls attempted.

Part 2 · The Six Sources of Truth

Every serious FMCG decision draws from one or more of these six data sources. Knowing which source answers which question is 80% of the analytical job.

# Source What it tells you Who owns it
1 Internal Sales (Primary + Secondary) Your actual business reality You / SFA system
2 Nielsen (retail audit) Retail-level share, distribution, offtake, competition Nielsen
3 Household Panel Consumer purchase behavior — the "who / how much / how often" Nielsen / Kantar
4 Brand Track Brand health — awareness, imagery, consideration Research agency
5 SOV & GRPs Media competitiveness — spend, reach, frequency Media agency
6 Usage & Attitude (U&A) Study The "why" behind consumer behavior Research agency

1. Internal Sales — the only reality

Everything else can look rosy, but if your internal secondary sales are collapsing, your brand is in trouble. Primary is what the company invoices to distributors. Secondary is what the distributor ships to the trade — and it is the single most important number to track because it determines what the consumer actually gets to see and buy.

2. Nielsen — the retail truth

Nielsen tells you how your brand is performing across the retail universe: market share, distribution, offtake, competitive benchmarking, brand health at the store level. It is the industry's single point of reference for competitive comparison.

3. Household Panel — the consumer truth

The panel is your consumer reality. It answers: Who buys you? How much? How often? What else do they buy? Demographics, town class, family composition — all sliceable. This is the source you use when you want to understand behavior, not just outcomes.

4. Brand Track — the mind truth

Brand track measures what your brand stands for in the consumer's head — brand awareness, ad awareness, share of endorsements, trial and repeat rates.

5. SOV & GRPs — the media truth

How much is competition spending? How often is your ad reaching your consumer? Share of Voice and Gross Rating Points quantify your media competitiveness.

6. Usage & Attitude Study — the "why"

The U&A is your bible for consumer behavior: how they use the product, why they use it, why they don't, where they buy, when they buy. Every launch or repositioning worth its salt starts here.

Part 3 · Reading Internal Numbers and Nielsen Together

Rules of the road for internal numbers

  • Chase growth, not absolutes. Absolute numbers matter, but growth is the metric that keeps you honest.
  • Separate value growth from volume growth. If you took a 10% price increase and sales grew 11%, you barely moved the needle. Consumers buy units, not rupees.
  • Always ask about the base effect. A previous-period 1+1 offer, a stock-out, a price hike — anything that distorted the base — will distort this month's growth number.
  • Map seasonality. Some categories (Glucon-D) do 60% of turnover in three months. Some brands double during festivals. Compare Q1-to-Q1, not Q1-to-Q2.
  • Get geography right. Internal sales come in ASM-territory buckets, which shift over time as territories are recut. Do not compare apples to reorganized apples.

The four internal KPIs to track

  1. Absolute Volume
  2. Absolute Value
  3. SKU / Variant Mix
  4. Volume & Value Growth

Drill-downs that matter

  • By SKU / Variant / Pack Type (bottles vs. sachets, jars vs. cartons)
  • By Geography — Urban vs. Rural, State, Metros, Tier 1 / 2 / 3 towns
  • By Channel — General Trade, Modern Trade, E-commerce

The five Nielsen KPIs I refuse to live without

# Metric Definition Why it matters
1 Market Share Value (MS Val) Your value sales ÷ total category value sales in a market. The headline share number every leadership deck opens with.
2 Market Share Volume (MS Vol) Your volume sales ÷ total category volume sales. Volume is the consumer reality; value is the pricing game.
3 Numeric Distribution (ND) Outlets where you are present ÷ total outlets where the category is sold. How wide are you?
4 Weighted Distribution (WtD) Category sales in your outlets ÷ total category sales. Are you present in the outlets that matter? Always chase this above ND.
5 Share Amongst Handlers (SAH) Your share only within the outlets that carry you. For a strong brand, SAH should be meaningfully higher than overall share.
A hypothetical to seal it: If your ND is 80% but your WtD is only 50%, you are present in a lot of outlets — but the wrong ones. Your feet are moving; your sales aren't. Fix quality of distribution, not just quantity.

Choosing the right unit for volume share

Hair colour → unit share (different formats, no common gram basis). Soaps → tonnage share (everything measures in grams). Pick whatever makes sense for the category — never blindly copy from one to the other.

Bringing Nielsen and Internal Numbers Together — the 2×2

The single most powerful diagnostic in FMCG is a 2×2 of Nielsen growth vs. Internal secondary growth. Every market you sell into plots somewhere on this chart. Interpret as follows:

  High Nielsen Growth Low Nielsen Growth
High Secondary Growth Doing Well — continue efforts, keep both engines running. Increase Investment — sales pipe is filling but consumer offtake isn't; you need more media, more consumer activation, more promo.
Low Secondary Growth Strong Brand — Push Sales — the consumer wants you but distribution is under-serving demand; strong sales plans help. Immediate Action Plan — nothing is working; go deep before you spend another rupee.

Two more reconciliation checks worth doing

  • Share-of-share ratio. Build a stable ratio of internal secondary volumes (or value, if you have only one SKU) to Nielsen offtake. Every time share moves in the wrong direction, first check whether this ratio has shifted — a change in pick-up rate often explains what looks like a share issue. Critical for new brands and Modern-Trade-heavy brands.
  • Geographical contribution mismatch. If your top 20 cities internally don't match your top 20 in Nielsen, wholesale is inflating internal numbers and your real consumer sits elsewhere. Your marketing needs to follow the consumer, not the invoice.
Golden rule: When marrying internal and Nielsen data, look for trends, not absolute matches. A 2–3% gap between an 8% internal growth and a 10% Nielsen growth is completely normal. If you chase absolute reconciliation, you will chase your own tail.

Part 4 · The Household Panel — Where Strategy Actually Gets Made

Nielsen retail audit tells you what is happening. The household panel tells you why. Retail audit is a scoreboard; panel is the game film. If you make brand decisions without panel, you are guessing.

1. Penetration

Penetration = Brand HHs ÷ Total Category HHs. Measure at two levels:

  • Category Penetration = Category HHs ÷ Universe. This tells you the lifecycle stage of the category. Soaps are near 100% penetration — you can only grow at competitors' expense, and loyalty is low. Hair colour is under 30% — category growth is the single biggest brand objective.
  • Brand Penetration = HHs your brand is bought into within the category. Set a realistic penetration objective (L'Oréal Excellence and Garnier Color Naturals cannot share the same target), then craft the marketing mix to hit and hold it. A drop in brand penetration is almost always a crisis — but measure over a quarter, never month-on-month.

2. Consumption

  • Total Consumption — total volume of your brand across all buying HHs.
  • Average Consumption — volume per HH. This is the number you watch to detect seasonality, promo lift, or ad impact. Ran a "use twice a day" campaign? Avg. consumption should move up.

3. Share of Requirement (SOR)

SOR = Your brand's volume in brand HHs ÷ total category volume in brand HHs. This is your share inside a household that already buys you. Because most HHs buy multiple brands (shampoo is the classic multi-brand category in India), SOR reveals how central you are to that HH.

  • SOR trending down → other brands are winning more occasions inside your consumer's home.
  • SOR trending up (especially for an occasional-use brand) → consumers are consolidating around you. Look for the enabler: distribution, pricing, affluence, communication.

4. Entry–Erosion Analysis: where growth comes from

For any period, decompose your buying HHs into three groups:

  • New Triers — HHs that bought you for the first time in the period. This is your trialability signal. Also check what % of your total consumption came from these new triers — are they meaningful buyers or just curious samplers? Use a period of at least 6 months; anything shorter is noise.
  • Retained Users — HHs that bought you in the previous period and this one. This is your loyalty. Watch the consumption trend within these HHs — are they doubling down on you or slowly adding a second brand to their repertoire?
  • Lapsers — HHs that bought you last period but not this one. High lapsage is a red flag, but it must always be judged inside the category context:
    • A baby-food brand for 6–9 month olds will demographically lose HHs every quarter — that is not a lapsage problem.
    • A summer brand like Glucon-D will look catastrophic if you compare Q2 to Q3 — the category simply isn't relevant.
    Also ask: were the lapsers heavy users or marginal buyers? Losing marginal buyers is inconvenient; losing heavy users is an emergency.

5. Gain / Loss Analysis: the four ways volume moves

  1. New Category Users / Category Lapsers — HHs entering or exiting the category through you. Baby products are the classic case.
  2. Increase / Decrease in Consumption — HHs simply using more or less of your brand. Beware of fill-rate changes: a shampoo brand that reduces sachet fill from 7.5 ml to 6 ml will see absolute consumption per HH drop, because HHs use one sachet at a time regardless. Conversely, a 10% extra-fill promo on a small SKU may not reduce pack purchase, so absolute consumption rises.
  3. Addition / Deletion to Repertoire — HHs adding you on top of existing brands to solve a specific job. Fabric softeners live here (they don't replace detergent). Ezee liquid detergent enters winter repertoires and exits in spring.
  4. Gain / Loss from Competition — direct substitution. This is the number that identifies your real competition — and it is often not who you thought.
The reality check no one wants to see: You launch a premium fairness cream, price it 200% above market, communicate to affluent skincare users — and panel shows your triers are actually coming from the bottom of the pyramid. That's either a media opportunity (pivot spend, embrace it) or a strategy failure (your positioning is not landing where you aimed). Either way, only panel will tell you before it is too late.

How I actually use panel — tactical playbook

Consumer promo evaluation

Panel gives you empirical evidence: did the promo bring in new HHs, deepen consumption in existing HHs, lift SOR, or steal from competition? Same questions for competitors' promos — so you know which mechanics work and which will hurt them most next quarter.

Cross promotion

Before you cross-promote with brand X, panel tells you the HH intersection: will you actually reach new HHs, or are you just talking to the same people twice?

SKU decisions

If All-India avg. consumption for your brand is 100 g but a particular state or SEC clocks 500 g, there is a real case for a 500 g SKU. You save packaging cost and you lock the consumer in longer — a big pack means the next brand-consideration event is further away.

Media deployment

If your intended TG was SEC A/B but your actual HHs are SEC C/D, take the media mass. Add mediums that reach your real consumer, not your imagined one.

Cannibalisation from a LUP (Low-Unit-Price) launch

The perennial FMCG debate — will a ₹5 sachet destroy the ₹50 bottle? Only panel can tell you, using competition and adjacent-category data, how much cannibalisation to expect. Guess this one wrong and you will destroy a profit pool overnight.

Bringing It All Together

Here is the mental model I keep coming back to:

  • Internal Secondary is your reality — start here, always.
  • Nielsen is your competitive scoreboard — reconcile trends, not absolutes.
  • Panel is your consumer film — reach for it when you need to explain why.
  • Brand Track, SOV/GRP, U&A are the diagnostics that convert insight into next quarter's plan.

Metrics don't make decisions. But without them, you make decisions on vibes. And vibes don't scale to ₹500-crore brands.
