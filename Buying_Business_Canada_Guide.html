Industries to AVOID (or approach with extreme caution)

  • Restaurants: 60% failure rate, thin margins (3–9%), high turnover, intense competition, perishable inventory
  • Retail clothing/fashion: E-commerce disruption, inventory risk, seasonal cash flow
  • Print media / bookstores: Structural decline
  • Taxi/limo services: Disrupted by rideshare (Uber/Lyft)
  • Travel agencies: Online booking has eliminated most need
  • Any business where revenue depends on ONE customer: Customer concentration > 30% is high risk

What Makes a Business Profitable to BUY (not just to run)?

  1. Recurring revenue: Contracts, subscriptions, maintenance agreements → predictable cash flow
  2. Owner-independent: Business runs without the current owner being there daily (systems, key staff in place)
  3. Diversified customer base: No single customer > 15% of revenue
  4. Growth potential: Untapped opportunities the current owner hasn't pursued (new markets, services, online)
  5. Strong cash flow relative to price: Target businesses generating 20–33% cash-on-cash return (price = 3–5× annual cash flow)
  6. Transferable value: Brand, customer relationships, licenses, systems — not just the owner's personal relationships

5. How to Evaluate a Business Before Buying

5.1 The 7 Critical Questions

  1. Why is the owner selling? — Retirement? Burnout? Declining industry? Divorce? Hidden problems?
  2. Can this business run without the current owner? — If revenue walks out with the owner, you're buying a job, not a business.
  3. Are the financials real? — Demand 3 years of tax returns (CRA-filed, not internal statements). Cash businesses often underreport.
  4. What's the trend? — Is revenue/profit growing, flat, or declining over 3 years?
  5. What am I actually buying? — Assets? Goodwill? Customer list? Real estate? Equipment? Employees?
  6. What could go wrong? — Key person risk, lease expiry, customer concentration, regulatory changes, technology disruption.
  7. What would I do differently? — If you can't identify 2–3 concrete growth levers, you may not be the right buyer.

5.2 Financial Analysis Framework

MetricWhat to Look ForRed Flag
Revenue trend3+ years of stable or growing revenueDeclining YoY, one-time spikes
Gross MarginConsistent with industry (30–60% for services, 20–40% for products)Margins declining, below industry average
SDE (Seller's Discretionary Earnings)True cash flow = Net income + owner salary + add-backs (personal expenses, one-time costs, depreciation)SDE declining; heavy add-backs that seem questionable
Cash flow consistencyMonthly cash flow should be relatively stable, not lumpyOne or two months carry the entire year
Customer concentrationNo single customer > 15% of revenueTop customer > 30% (business at risk if they leave)
Working capitalEnough to cover 2–3 months of operationsNegative working capital, overdue payables
Debt & liabilitiesClean balance sheet, known debtsHidden liabilities, tax arrears, pending lawsuits

5.3 Key Add-Backs for SDE Calculation

SDE = Net Income + Owner's Salary + Owner Benefits + Interest + Depreciation + One-Time Expenses

Common legitimate add-backs:
  • Owner's total compensation (salary, bonuses, benefits)
  • Personal expenses run through the business (car, phone, travel, meals)
  • One-time legal or professional fees
  • Non-cash expenses (depreciation, amortization)
  • Above-market rent (if owner also owns the building)
  • Family members on payroll who don't actually work

7. Due Diligence Checklist

Financial Due Diligence

  • 3 years of CRA-filed tax returns (T2 corporate or T1 sole proprietor)
  • 3 years of financial statements (income statement, balance sheet, cash flow)
  • Monthly bank statements for past 24 months
  • Accounts receivable aging report (who owes money and how old)
  • Accounts payable list (who is owed money)
  • GST/HST returns (verify reported revenue matches tax filings)
  • Payroll records and T4 summaries
  • List of all assets with depreciation schedule
  • Outstanding debts, loans, liens, or judgments
  • Verify no CRA arrears (ask seller to provide CRA clearance certificate)

Operational Due Diligence

  • Customer list with revenue per customer (check concentration)
  • Supplier agreements and pricing terms
  • Employee list: roles, tenure, compensation, key-person identification
  • Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) documented?
  • Technology systems / software in use
  • Inventory count and valuation method
  • Insurance policies (general liability, E&O, property)

Legal Due Diligence

  • Commercial lease agreement (term, renewal options, assignment clause)
  • All contracts (customer, vendor, employee, non-competes)
  • Intellectual property (trademarks, patents, domains, branding)
  • Licenses and permits (verify they are transferable)
  • Pending or historical litigation
  • Environmental compliance (especially manufacturing, gas stations)
  • Zoning compliance for the business location
  • Non-compete agreement from seller (typically 3–5 years, geographic scope)

Market & Competition Due Diligence

  • Industry growth rate and outlook
  • Competitive landscape — who are the top 3 competitors?
  • Online reputation (Google reviews, BBB, social media)
  • Market share position
  • Regulatory changes that could affect the business
NEVER skip due diligence. Budget $5,000–$25,000 for professional due diligence (lawyer + accountant). It saves you from buying a disaster. Walk away if the seller refuses to provide clean financials.

Typical Deal Structure

Common acquisition financing stack:
  • Down payment (buyer cash): 10–30% of purchase price
  • Seller financing (VTB): 10–30% over 3–5 years
  • Bank/BDC loan: 40–60% of purchase price, secured by business assets
  • Earnout: 5–20% contingent on future performance (bridges valuation gaps)
Example: $500K business → $100K cash + $100K seller note (5yr) + $250K BDC loan + $50K earnout

9. Legal Structure & Requirements

Asset Purchase vs. Share Purchase

FactorAsset PurchaseShare Purchase
What you buySpecific assets (equipment, inventory, goodwill, customer list)Ownership shares of the corporation
LiabilitiesGenerally do NOT assume past liabilitiesInherit ALL liabilities (known and unknown)
Tax (Buyer)✅ Better — can depreciate/amortize purchased assets (CCA deductions)❌ Worse — no step-up in asset values
Tax (Seller)❌ Worse — may face recapture, loses capital gains exemption eligibility✅ Better — Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption ($1.25M in 2026)
Contracts/LeasesMust be individually assigned/renegotiatedAuto-transfer with the company (unless change-of-control clause)
EmployeesTechnically new employer — may need to re-hireEmployment continues seamlessly
ComplexityMore complex (asset-by-asset transfer)Simpler (one transaction)
RecommendationUsually preferred by BUYERS (cleaner, tax benefits)Preferred by SELLERS (LCGE, clean exit)
Negotiation point: Buyers almost always prefer asset purchases. Sellers prefer share purchases. The price often bridges this gap — sellers accept asset purchase structure in exchange for a higher price.

Key Legal Documents in a Business Purchase

  1. Letter of Intent (LOI) / Term Sheet: Non-binding outline of deal terms
  2. Non-Disclosure Agreement (NDA): Before receiving confidential financial information
  3. Asset/Share Purchase Agreement (APA/SPA): The main contract — 30–80 pages typically
  4. Non-Compete / Non-Solicitation Agreement: Seller can't start a competing business or poach staff/customers
  5. Transition Services Agreement: Seller stays for 30–90 days to transition knowledge
  6. Commercial Lease Assignment: Landlord consent to transfer the lease to buyer
  7. Employment Agreements: For key staff you want to retain
  8. Promissory Note: If seller financing is part of the deal

10. Step-by-Step Process: From Search to Close

1
Self-Assessment (Week 1–2)
Define: What industries do you know? What's your budget? How hands-on do you want to be? What's your lifestyle goal? Complete BDC's free business readiness assessment.
2
Build Your Team (Week 2–3)
Engage: (1) Business lawyer, (2) CPA/accountant experienced in acquisitions, (3) Optionally a business broker for buy-side advisory. Get pre-approved for financing from BDC or your bank.
3
Search & Filter (Week 3–12)
Browse all platforms listed in Section 1. Set alerts. Network — tell everyone you're looking. 80% of best deals are off-market (word of mouth, direct approach to owners). Target 20 listings → shortlist 5.
4
Initial Screening (Per business: 1–2 weeks)
Sign NDA → receive financials. Run quick SDE calculation. Ask the 7 Critical Questions (Section 5.1). Reject quickly if red flags appear. Spend time with top 2–3 candidates.
5
Letter of Intent (LOI)
Submit non-binding offer with: price range, deal structure, due diligence period (45–90 days), key conditions. LOI gives you exclusivity to investigate further.
6
Due Diligence (45–90 days)
Execute the full checklist in Section 7. Lawyer reviews contracts, accountant verifies financials, you observe operations. Visit the business multiple times. Talk to key employees (with seller's permission).
7
Valuation & Price Negotiation
Complete your valuation using methods in Section 6. Present findings to seller with justification. Negotiate price, terms, seller financing, non-compete, transition period.
8
Purchase Agreement & Financing
Lawyer drafts APA/SPA. Finalize bank/BDC loan. Sign all documents. Satisfy closing conditions (lease assignment, license transfers, employee agreements).
9
Closing Day
Funds transfer, keys handed over, seller announces transition to staff and customers, you become the owner. Typically a brief celebration followed by Day 1 of operations.
10
Transition Period (30–90 days)
Seller stays to introduce you to key relationships, transfer knowledge, and ensure smooth handover. Focus on listening and learning — don't make big changes in the first 30 days.
Timeline: From initial search to closing typically takes 6–12 months. Don't rush — the cost of buying the wrong business far exceeds the cost of being patient.

11. Red Flags & Common Mistakes

Red Flags in a Business for Sale

  1. Seller won't provide tax returns — Only shows "internal" P&L. Likely underreporting to CRA.
  2. Revenue heavily dependent on the owner's personal relationships — Revenue may leave when they do.
  3. Lease expiring soon with no renewal option — Landlord can refuse or dramatically raise rent.
  4. Declining revenue with no clear explanation — "It just needs marketing" is not a valid reason.
  5. Key employees threatening to leave — Interview them early (with seller's consent).
  6. Pending litigation or regulatory issues — Lawyer must search for these.
  7. Asking price > 5× SDE for a small business — Unless there's exceptional growth, this is overpriced.
  8. Seller refuses non-compete agreement — They may plan to open across the street.
  9. Environmental liabilities — Gas stations, dry cleaners, manufacturing sites can have $100K+ cleanup costs.
  10. "Upside potential" used to justify high price — You're buying current cash flow, not futures. Pay for what IS, not what COULD be.

Common Buyer Mistakes

  • Falling in love with the business before completing due diligence
  • Not getting independent professional advice (using seller's lawyer/accountant)
  • Overpaying because of emotional attachment or fear of losing the deal
  • Underestimating working capital needs — You need cash reserves beyond the purchase price
  • Not negotiating seller financing — Always ask. 60–70% of small business sales include VTB.
  • Ignoring culture and employees — The business IS the people in many cases
  • Making big changes Day 1 — Listen first, change later (after 90 days)

12. Tax Considerations

Key Tax Concepts for Buyers

ConceptExplanationImpact
CCA (Capital Cost Allowance) Tax depreciation on purchased assets (equipment, vehicles, goodwill) In asset purchase, buyer allocates price across asset classes → tax deductions over time
Goodwill (Class 14.1) Intangible value (brand, customer relationships, reputation) 5% CCA per year — slow deduction but still valuable
GST/HST on Purchase Business assets generally subject to GST/HST Use joint Section 167 election to transfer without GST — saves cash flow at closing
LCGE (Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption) $1.25M (2026) of tax-free capital gains on sale of qualifying small business shares Seller wants share sale to access LCGE. Buyer can compensate with higher price in asset deal.
Purchase Price Allocation How the total price is split between assets (equipment, inventory, goodwill, non-compete) Buyer wants more allocated to fast-depreciating assets; seller wants more to goodwill/shares. Must be negotiated.
Critical: Get your accountant involved BEFORE signing. Tax structuring can save or cost you tens of thousands of dollars. The purchase price allocation in the APA is a negotiation point.

Key Considerations for Newcomers

  • Credit history: You won't have Canadian credit. BDC is more flexible than Big 5 banks for new immigrants.
  • Local knowledge: Partner with a Canadian advisor or broker who understands the local market.
  • Cultural differences: Canadian business culture values transparency, written agreements, and relationship-building before transactions.
  • Language: If English/French isn't your first language, invest in a bilingual lawyer for contract review.
  • Community: Explore BDC's newcomer entrepreneur resources: bdc.ca/en/i-am/newcomer-entrepreneur

Representations & warrantiesSeller guarantees the truth of their disclosures — you have legal recourse if they lied
Golden Rule of Negotiation: Focus on the TOTAL deal economics, not just the headline price. A $500K deal with 30% seller financing at 5% interest and a 90-day transition is BETTER than a $450K all-cash deal with a 2-week transition and no non-compete.

16. After the Purchase: First 90 Days

Day 1–30: Listen & Learn

  • Meet every employee one-on-one — understand their role, concerns, and ideas
  • Call top 10 customers personally — introduce yourself, ask what they value
  • Observe operations without changing anything
  • Understand the cash flow cycle — when money comes in and goes out
  • Identify the "owner's manual" — SOPs, vendor contacts, passwords, routines
  • Meet key suppliers and confirm pricing/terms continue

Day 31–60: Stabilize & Quick Wins

  • Fix any immediate operational pain points employees have been asking about
  • Set up your own accounting/bookkeeping system — don't rely on seller's
  • Review all contracts with your lawyer — lease, suppliers, employees
  • Implement basic KPI tracking if none exists (revenue/day, customer count, margins)
  • Start building your personal relationship with the top 20% of customers who drive 80% of revenue

Day 61–90: Optimize & Grow

  • Implement 1–2 improvements you identified during due diligence
  • Assess staff — retain A-players, develop B-players, address C-players
  • Review pricing — most small businesses are underpricing their services
  • Build a 12-month plan with specific revenue and profit targets
  • Set up proper insurance (E&O, general liability, key person)
  • Join industry association and local business network (BNI, Chamber of Commerce)
The #1 mistake new owners make: Changing too much too fast. The business was profitable before you arrived. Understand WHY before changing HOW.
